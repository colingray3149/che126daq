Needs for Lithium-ion Battery Runaway Containment
Application: all-electric, battery-powered passenger aircraft (commonly categorized as electric
Vertical Take-Off and Landing [eVTOL], urban air mobility [UAM], or regional electric aircraft)
   1.​ Minimal Volumetric & Gravimetric Weight Penalty: Every kilogram added reduces
        payload capacity or increases fuel burn. High volumetric energy density is vital for
        space-constrained avionics bays or EFB battery cases.
   2.​ Modularity: Modern aircraft maintenance relies on Line Replaceable Units (LRUs). If a
        single cell degrades or experiences a localized fault, airlines refuse to scrap an entire
        $50,000+ battery pack. They require the ability to swap out sub-modules at the gate or in
        regional maintenance hangars.
   3.​ RTCA DO-160G: Battery Safety at extreme conditions (Environmental Conditions
        and Test Procedures for Airborne Equipment)
            a.​ Thermal Cycling (DO-160G Section 4 & 5): Validates that inter-cell thermal
                barriers do not crack, delaminate, or lose structural integrity when rapidly cycled
                between extreme cold (-55 C at altitude) and peak operating heat (+85^ C).
            b.​ Multi-Axis Vibration & Structural Fatigue (DO-160G Section 8): Tests
                equipment against continuous low-frequency and high-frequency airframe
                vibrations (e.g., engine rumble, turbulent flight load) over hundreds of hours. This
                ensures that thin aerogel blankets or mica plates do not pulverize into dust or shift
                out of position over time.
            c.​ Operational Shock & Crash Safety (DO-160G Section 7): Ensures the
                structural housing and inter-cell barriers maintain containment during high-G
                events (e.g., hard landings, sudden deceleration, or turbulence).
   4.​ The Flammability Requirement (FAR Part 25): Every structural material, thermal
        barrier (e.g., aerogel pads, mica sheets, silicone foam), housing, and potting compound
        used inside an avionics bay or battery enclosure must pass strict vertical burn tests (e.g.,
        60-second or 12-second direct flame exposure) to ensure they are self-extinguishing and
        do not release toxic fumes or flame drips.
   5.​ Low-cost: minimize added $/kWh and aircraft system cost.
            a.​ Economic constraint: Battery-powered flight must achieve cost parity with
                conventional aircraft, accounting for battery production and replacement,
                electricity costs, and jet-fuel costs.
   6.​ For insurers: there is likely some threshold of guarantee, like an actual percentage, that
        insurers require the battery to not explode in order for them to be willing to insure it. Or,
        the insurance would just be insanely expensive.

1. Electrical isolation: stopping “current dumping” between parallel cells

Your list only addresses heat. In parallel-connected packs, a failing cell can also be fed by its
neighbors’ current. One study found that packs with electrically connected cells propagated
thermal runaway because neighboring cells dumped current into the failing one, while packs with
electrically disconnected cells did not propagate and neighbors stayed below 85 °C. That work
led to the first battery design with individual cell fusing built into the current collectors. The
approach adds very little mass. A related idea is thermally disconnecting busbars, since copper
busbars between tightly packed modules can become a heat transfer path during a fire.​

2. Endothermic “sacrificial” chemistry (different from PCMs)

Instead of melting, these materials decompose chemically and absorb heat at runaway
temperatures. Aluminum hydroxide, for example, releases its crystal water when it decomposes,
absorbing heat, and leaves behind porous alumina that then acts as an insulator. Decomposition
onset can be matched to the runaway window: about 200 °C for aluminum hydroxide and about
330 °C for magnesium hydroxide. This gives you a heat sink and an insulator in one material,


===== PDF PAGE 9 =====
which could be a low-mass complement to your thermally responsive material idea. GM has a
patent on this approach.​
 https://patents.justia.com/patent/20240106021 usptouspto

3. Intumescent materials

These are distinct from ceramic coatings because they swell into an insulating char only when
heated, so they are thin and light during normal operation. In an early Tesla patent, uncoated cells
propagated runaway to the next cell within 3 to 4 minutes, while intumescent-coated cells did not
propagate. A Warwick study comparing five barriers found a stainless steel barrier coated with
intumescent flame-retardant fabric performed best, and sidewall rupture counts correlated
linearly with propagation rate. That makes it relevant if you’re using high-energy cylindrical
cells.​
 https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/186891 usptowarwick

4. Flame arrestors and quenching meshes in the vent path

This addresses your altitude and venting gap directly. A conductive mesh over the vent cools
exiting gas below its auto-ignition temperature, and the required hole size depends on the gas’s
auto-ignition temperature and velocity. There is a trade-off you’ll care about at low pressure:
smaller openings reject more heat but increase the forces on the mesh during a vent. A 2025 BIT
study designed a multilayer quenching plate and found that front-facing configurations needed
five layers at 75% SOC and seven at 100% SOC, while side-facing configurations needed only
three. Re-evaluating that layer count at altitude-level gas velocities could be a well-scoped
8-week question.​

5. Self-triggered suppression microcapsules

This is a passive alternative to your “borrow coolant from the aircraft” idea, with no plumbing.
The capsules rupture at 130 °C and release Novec 1230, which extinguishes flames, and
HFO-1336mzz(Z), which provides cooling; together they delayed runaway and inhibited
propagation. Earlier UMD work found that Novec 1230 at 15.2 vol.% significantly inhibited
combustion of ejected material and prevented complete propagation through fully charged cell
arrays.​
 https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2025/ta/d5ta03776j rscumd

6. Oxygen exclusion and inerting

This approach removes oxygen from the fire triangle instead of fighting heat. In one cited test,
propagation occurred at normal 21% oxygen, but no significant propagation to adjacent cells
occurred when oxygen was reduced to about 11.3%. The caveat is that nitrogen doesn’t instantly
penetrate sealed cells, so inerting suppresses the fire around the cell rather than the runaway
inside it. A sealed module with a one-way vent, which keeps oxygen out while letting gas
escape, is a lighter variant of the same idea.​

7. Two-phase dielectric immersion

Cells sit in a fluid that boils at a chosen temperature. In Carrar’s test, a 72 Ah pouch cell was
driven above 800 °C while the adjacent cell stayed at about 50 °C, and the company says the
system works passively, without sensors or suppression systems. The fluid mass is the obvious
problem for your weight budget, but it is a useful benchmark to compare against.​

8. Heat pipes that vent and become insulators

This is closest to your thermal-switch concept. In this patent, a heat pipe cools cells normally.
After a thermal event, a pressure-relief device lets it dump its working fluid to become a vented
heat pipe, which then inhibits heat transfer to the next cell. So it conducts heat when things are
normal and insulates during a failure, using existing hardware.​
 https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9689624 uspto

9. Intra-cell safety: stopping the first cell from running away

Stanford developed a current-collector coating that shuts the battery down under overheating or
shorting and lets it resume normal function afterward, with a switching temperature that can be
tuned. On the electrolyte side, a nonflammable localized high-concentration electrolyte raised the
runaway trigger temperature by 47.3 °C and lowered the peak runaway temperature by 71.8 °C.
A caution: a Nature Communications study found that non-flammable concentrated electrolytes
could not prevent runaway, so “nonflammable” does not mean “safe.”​

11. Off-gas detection before runaway

This is a simpler, off-the-shelf version of your “sensors in vent channels” idea. In third-party
testing, an off-gas sensor gave 6.4 minutes of warning before runaway, and stopping the charge
when it alarmed avoided runaway entirely. Notably, a hydrogen sensor only detected gas at the
moment of runaway. This pairs naturally with your “active discharge” idea, because the warning
gives the system time to act.​

Research into mitigating lithium-ion battery thermal runaway propagation has increasingly focused on
multi-functional hybrid systems that pair static thermal insulation with active enthalpy absorption or
dynamic thermal regulation.

    ●​ Aerogel + Phase Change Material (PCM) Composites
          ○​ Mechanism: Combining nanoporous silica or nanofiber aerogel matrices impregnated
              with organic/inorganic phase change materials (CPCMs). The PCM acts as an active
              latent heat sink during early-stage thermal spikes, while the inorganic aerogel framework
              prevents liquid leakage and provides permanent low thermal conductivity (approx 0.013 -
              0.018 W/m*K) once phase transition occurs.
          ○​ Literature Reference: Mei et al. (2023) developed organic-inorganic hybrid CPCM
              aerogel matrices, demonstrating effective suppression of thermal propagation. Similarly,
              Wong et al. (2024) evaluated nanofiber aerogel composite materials integrated with
              phase-absorption layers to delay time intervals between runaway events in high-energy
              modules.
    ●​ Temperature-Responsive Polymer Switch + Insulation Interlayers


===== PDF PAGE 13 =====
           ○​ Mechanism: Advanced thermal-switching materials that dynamically alter their thermal
              conductivity (transitioning from a conductive state during normal operation to an
              adiabatic insulating state during high-temperature anomalies).
           ○​ Literature Reference: Wang et al. (2024) designed a rapid temperature-responsive thermal
              regulator that prevents up to 80% of heat transmission during thermal runaway while
              maintaining uniform temperature distribution under normal working conditions.

2. Phase-Change Material (PCM) + Intumescent Polymer (Enthalpy Buffer + Expandable Shield)

   ●​ How They Work Together: Encapsulate cells in a composite PCM wax/salt matrix, and coat
      module divider walls with an intumescent polymer coating.
   ●​ Synergy: The PCM acts as an active heat sink during early-stage thermal spikes, absorbing latent
      thermal energy at a constant temperature before thermal runaway fully triggers. If the trigger cell
      exceeds the PCM's heat capacity and ignites, the extreme heat causes the intumescent coating to
      expand up to 100×, creating a secondary char barrier that blocks runaway flame propagation to
      the next cell tier.
   ●​ Primary Application: Dense pouch or cylindrical cell arrays. explored in advanced thermal
      protection systems, particularly for dense battery packs and aerospace applications where space is
      tightly constrained.
   ●​ Weaknesses: Significant volumetric expansion requires mechanical spacing allowances within
      the module design. Liquid PCM leakage risks and chemical incompatibility between organic
      waxes and polymer binder matrices can also complicate manufacturing.
   ●​ Unexplored Areas: Long-term thermal-cycling durability under repeated charge/discharge loads
      (where repeated melting and solidifying of the PCM can degrade surrounding intumescent
      bonding integrity) remains underexplored in literature.

3. Ceramic Fiber Paper + Steel Structural Framing (Thermal Separation + Mechanical
Containment)

   ●​ How They Work Together: Line steel compartment walls or structural module frames with
      compressible aluminosilicate ceramic fiber paper.
   ●​ Synergy: Steel provides high structural rigidity, containment strength, and blast resistance, but its
      high thermal conductivity causes heat to rapidly transfer to neighboring compartments. Thin
      ceramic fiber paper insulates the steel surfaces, preventing thermal bridging while retaining the
      structural enclosure benefits.
   ●​ Primary Application: Sub-module bulkheads and module housing frames.
   ●​ Weaknesses: Ceramic fiber papers are friable, prone to shedding particulates under vibrational
      stress, and add non-active weight that degrades the gravimetric energy density of the battery pack.
   ●​ Unexplored Areas: Optimization of ultra-thin, binder-modified ceramic papers that maintain
      high compression resilience under high-vibration aerospace or EV operating environments while
      completely eliminating particulate dusting.

4. Intumescent Aerogel Composites (Integrated Dual Barrier)

    ●​ How They Work Together: A single manufactured composite material featuring a nanoporous
       aerogel core impregnated or laminated with intumescent expanding compounds.
    ●​ Synergy: Combines the low static thermal conductivity of aerogel with dynamic thermal
       insulation—as temperatures exceed 200°C, the intumescent component expands to fill air gaps
       left by cell swelling or geometric deformation during runaway.
    ●​ Weaknesses: High production costs, multi-step chemical synthesis complexity, and potential
       brittleness of the aerogel framework under mechanical shear stress.
    ●​ Unexplored Areas: Scalable, low-cost impregnation techniques for embedding intumescent
       nanoparticles directly into flexible aerogel blankets without destroying the delicate 3D
       nanoporous pore structure.

              𝐴𝑙(𝑂𝐻)3 → 𝐴𝑙2𝑂3 + 3𝐻2𝑂) upon reaching high temperatures, and mica deflects direct
             high-velocity plasma/flame jets.
    ●​ Weaknesses:
         ○​ Increased thickness and weight penalty; potential manufacturing challenges in uniformly
             distributing aluminum hydroxide within brittle aerogel structures.

2. Phase Change Material (PCM) + Intumescent Coating + Basalt ("Lava Rock") Fiber Wrap
    ●​ Concept: Encapsulating cells in a PCM matrix while wrapping the module assembly in a basalt
       fiber fabric coated with an intumescent paint.
    ●​ Strengths:
           ○​ Basalt fiber offers superior radiant heat resistance and structural integrity under fire
                conditions. If the PCM saturates and a cell breaches, the intumescent coating expands up
                to 100 × to seal air gaps and trap expanding gases.
    ●​ Weaknesses:
           ○​ Significant volumetric expansion requires dedicated mechanical spacing tolerances;
                heavy multi-layer setup reduces pack gravimetric energy density.

3. Aluminosilicate Ceramic Fiber Paper + Aluminum Hydroxide Slurry Impregnation
    ●​ Concept: Impregnating porous high-temperature ceramic fiber paper with an endothermic
       aluminum hydroxide binder solution.

    ●​ Strengths:
           ○​ Ceramic fiber maintains structural stability above 1, 200°𝐶, while the sacrificial
              endothermic chemistry absorbs massive thermal energy during decomposition.
    ●​ Weaknesses:
           ○​ Ceramic fiber papers are friable and prone to shedding particulate matter; water vapor
              release from aluminum hydroxide during accidental mild overheating could potentially
              interact with compromised cell packaging.

4. Basalt ("Lava Rock") Fiber Fabric + Mica Plate Lamination
    ●​ Concept: Mechanically stitching or bonding basalt fiber textile layers directly to rigid phlogopite
       mica plates.
    ●​ Strengths:
           ○​ High flexibility, ease of cutting/prototyping, and exceptional resistance to open flames

    ●​ Weaknesses:
           ○​ Lacks intrinsic low thermal conductivity (both basalt and mica conduct heat faster than
               aerogels) and provides zero active latent heat buffering.

Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP) & Lightweight
Composites
Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP) and lightweight composite materials are heavily utilized in
aerospace engineering due to their exceptional strength-to-weight ratios. When applied to lithium-ion
battery containment and thermal runaway prevention, they serve as lightweight structural housings or
localized cell sleeves.


Have Been Done
    1.​ CFRP Structural Enclosures + Internal Aerogel / Mica Blankets
          ○​ How They Work Together: The aircraft battery module uses a lightweight molded
              CFRP outer box or tray for primary structural rigidity, lined internally with thin
              nanoporous silica aerogel sheets and phlogopite mica plates.
          ○​ Strengths: CFRP provides high specific strength and stiffness without the heavy
              gravimetric penalty of steel or thick aluminum, while the internal aerogel and mica layers
              provide thermal insulation and flame deflection.
          ○​ Weaknesses: CFRP matrices are organic polymer-based and can degrade, delaminate, or
              burn when subjected to prolonged direct high-temperature flame jets ( >600 C) unless
              protected by an inner thermal barrier.
    2.​ NASA-Style CFRP Cell Sleeves / Wraps
          ○​ How They Work Together: Individual cylindrical cells are wrapped in a thin,
              custom-engineered carbon fiber sleeve infused with flame-retardant epoxy matrices.
          ○​ Strengths: Prevents sidewall ruptures (SWR) of small cylindrical cells, adds minimal
              mass compared to metal housings, and is non-electrically conductive depending on the
              weave and matrix sizing.

Not Yet Been Done (Unexplored or Emerging Areas)
    1.​ Intumescent-Impregnated CFRP Matrix Composites
            ○​ Concept: Embedding expandable intumescent microcapsules directly into the epoxy
               resin matrix of the structural CFRP weave during manufacturing.
            ○​ Strengths: During normal flight, the CFRP functions purely as a high-strength structural
               skin. If a localized thermal runaway spike occurs, the embedded intumescent compounds
               activate, swelling internally to choke off oxygen and create a thick carbonaceous char
               barrier within the composite plies themselves.
            ○​ Weaknesses: Introducing foreign compounds into the epoxy matrix can compromise the
               interlaminar shear strength and mechanical fatigue life of the composite under cyclic
               aircraft loading.
    2.​ CFRP + Aerogel-Core Sandwich Panels with Integrated Micro-Venting Channels
            ○​ Concept: A structural sandwich panel consisting of thin CFRP face sheets enclosing a
               core made of intumescent aerogel composite, integrated with micro-machined venting
               flutes that safely channel off-gases overboard.
            ○​ Strengths: Offers a multi-functional structural wall that simultaneously handles
               mechanical crash loads, provides ultra-low thermal insulation, and acts as a
               pressure-relief duct manifold for aviation environments.
            ○​ Weaknesses: Extremely complex and costly multi-step manufacturing process; difficult
               to nondestructively inspect for internal micro-delamination after vibrational stress.

Key Trade-Offs for Aviation (eVTOL / Commuter Aircraft)
   ●​ Thermal-Structural Conflict: While CFRP provides exceptional weight savings to help reach
       pack targets (≈ 650 𝑊ℎ/𝑘𝑔), standard polymer matrices lose structural integrity at elevated
       temperatures ( >300- 400 C).
   ●​ Regulatory Compliance: Any CFRP housing or internal composite barrier used in passenger
       aircraft must satisfy strict flammability, smoke, and toxicity (FST) mandates such as FAR 25.853
       and UL 94 V-0, requiring specialized flame-retardant resin systems (e.g., modified cyanate esters
       or high-temperature epoxy syntactics).

Combination 1: Structural-Thermal-Flame Barrier

Materials: CFRP Plate + Nanoporous Silica Aerogel Sheet + Phlogopite Mica Plate

Material-by-Material Reasoning

   1.​ Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP) Plate
          ○​ Reasoning: Serves as the primary structural housing and load-bearing
             inter-module wall. Unlike heavy steel or aluminum, CFRP provides an
             exceptional specific strength-to-weight ratio, ensuring the battery pack satisfies
             aviation gravimetric energy density goals without compromising structural
             rigidity.


===== PDF PAGE 19 =====
2.​ Nanoporous Silica Aerogel Sheet
       ○​ Reasoning: Implemented as the core static thermal insulator. With extreme
          porosity (≈99%) and an ultra-low thermal conductivity (k≈0.013–0.018 W/m⋅K),
          it blocks conductive heat flux to keep adjacent cell skin temperatures safely below
          the 150∘C non-propagation threshold during a thermal event.
3.​ Phlogopite Mica Plate
       ○​ Reasoning: Acts as a sacrificial high-temperature (>1,000∘C) flame and plasma
          shield. Raw aerogels can erode under direct high-velocity particle ejecta; the mica
          plate deflects incandescent jets and provides essential dielectric insulation against
          electrical arcing.
●​ Strengths:
       ○​ Exceptional weight-to-strength performance tailored for aerospace structural
          demands.
       ○​ Robust multi-stage defense against both slow conductive heat transfer and
          high-velocity plasma jets.
       ○​ Cell Deformation Behavior: The rigid exterior Carbon Fiber Reinforced
          Polymer (CFRP) housing combined with internal mica plates provides a strong
          mechanical box structure. This constrains normal cell swelling and prevents
          neighboring cells from bulging into one another during high-rate aviation
          discharge cycles.
       ○​ Post-Crash Survivability Probability: Very High: CFRP offers an exceptional
          strength-to-weight ratio that absorbs high-G crash impulses without yielding or
          adding excessive dead weight (preserving the ≈ 650𝑊ℎ/𝑘𝑔 pack target). The
          outer composite housing prevents the battery array from breaking apart or
          deforming into a "bomb" structure during a crash event, satisfying DO-160G
          crash safety requirements while the inner aerogel and mica layers maintain
          thermal stability.
●​ Weaknesses:
       ○​ CFRP polymer matrices degrade if exposed directly to prolonged temperatures
          above 200∘C–300∘C without protective facings.
       ○​ Rigid mica and CFRP plates require precise mechanical tolerances to
          accommodate normal cell swelling. Because the CFRP and mica layers are rigid,
          they lack high elasticity. If a cell undergoes severe swelling or mechanical
          deformation during early-stage abuse, rigid plates can create localized pressure
          pinch-points, potentially accelerating internal short circuits.

Combination 2: Enthalpy-Dynamic-Cushion Barrier

Materials: PCM Composite Matrix + Intumescent Polymer Coating + Compressible Ceramic
Fiber Paper

Material-by-Material Reasoning

   1.​ Phase-Change Material (PCM) Composite Matrix
          ○​ Reasoning: Functions as an active latent heat sink. During heavy aircraft
              discharge cycles (e.g., 3C–5C takeoff/climb) or early thermal spikes, the PCM
              absorbs massive amounts of thermal energy nearly isothermally via phase
              transition, smoothing out operating temperatures.
   2.​ Intumescent Polymer Coating
          ○​ Reasoning: Provides dynamic thermal expansion. When subjected to temperatures
              exceeding critical thresholds (≈200∘C), the intumescent binder swells up to 100×
              its original volume, generating a thick carbonaceous char layer that autonomously
              seals air gaps created by cell deformation.
   3.​ Compressible Aluminosilicate Ceramic Fiber Paper
          ○​ Reasoning: Acts as a high-temperature structural cushion and thermal break. It
              withstands continuous temperatures above 1,000∘C while absorbing multi-axis
              aircraft vibrations (DO-160G Section 8) and buffering mechanical expansion
              cycles.
   ●​ Strengths:
          ○​ Dynamic gap-filling and active enthalpy buffering prevent thermal spikes from
              escalating into full runaway.
          ○​ Compressible ceramic layers absorb mechanical shock and vibration effectively
              under flight conditions.
          ○​ Cell Deformation Behavior: Highly compliant. The compressible ceramic fiber
              paper and soft PCM/intumescent matrix act as mechanical buffers. They naturally
              absorb and accommodate the breathing, expansion, and minor geometrical
              deformations of pouch or prismatic cells over their operational lifetime.
   ●​ Weaknesses:


===== PDF PAGE 21 =====
         ○​ PCMs add non-active weight and present potential liquid leakage risks if
            encapsulation fails under extreme mechanical shock.
         ○​ Intumescent expansion requires dedicated void space within the module
            architecture to prevent over-pressurization.
         ○​ Under massive structural impacts or crashes, soft or semi-fluid materials provide
            virtually zero structural rigidity on their own, leaving the cells vulnerable to
            crushing unless housed in a rigid secondary frame.
   ●​ Post-Crash Survivability Probability: Moderate.
         ○​ Reasoning: While this combination excels at internal thermal management and
            local gap-sealing via intumescent expansion (100 ×), it lacks structural
            load-bearing capacity. Under a severe crash impulse (20𝑔), a module relying
            solely on soft PCMs and ceramic paper risks bulk structural collapse, potentially
            causing widespread mechanical deformation across the cell array unless backed
            by an external aerospace-grade frame.

          ○​ 100% inorganic composition ensures zero toxic smoke emission or organic binder
             degradation during a fire event.
          ○​ Exceptional flexibility and vibration resistance tailored for dynamic aerospace

   ●​ Post-Crash Survivability Probability: Moderate-Low (As a standalone housing).
          ○​ Reasoning: While completely inorganic and non-flammable under extreme flame

             achieve high survivability probability in an aircraft, this flexible blanket must be
             paired with an outer lightweight structural frame (such as a thin CFRP or titanium
             exoskeleton) to prevent catastrophic pack deformation during a crash landing.

Hardware details

Fixture. The test sits in a cinderblock cavity, and the cinderblock sits in a cardboard box. The
bottom of the cinderblock is sealed with plastic wrap and duct tape to keep the sand in. The
barrier is clamped vertically in the middle of the cavity, dividing it into a hot side and a cold side.
Both sides are filled with sand. Make the barrier span the full width of the cavity, or at least
extend well past the heater and battery on every edge. Otherwise heat can travel through the sand
around the barrier instead of through it.

Sand. Use dry sand. Damp sand absorbs heat as its water evaporates, which would make every
barrier look better than it is. Use the same mass of sand on each side every run, and pack it the
same way each time. Sand conducts better when packed tightly, so inconsistent packing adds
scatter to your results. Let the sand cool fully between runs, or swap in fresh room-temperature
sand. Sand holds a lot of heat.

Hot side. A high-temperature cartridge heater, rated for at least 700 °C, is pressed directly
against the barrier’s hot face. Power it through a variac. Since the variac is manual, you set the
temperature by adjusting voltage while watching the heater thermocouple. Note your variac
setting for each run. Ramp to about 600 °C and hold for 30 minutes. A real runaway spikes
within seconds and then decays, so holding at peak is conservative. Report your ramp rate and
the manual control as limitations of the method.

A bare cartridge heater only touches the barrier along a narrow strip, so it heats a line rather than
a whole face the way a failing cell would. Note this in your report. Cartridge heaters also rely on


===== PDF PAGE 33 =====
their surroundings to carry heat away, and sand does this poorly. Bring the voltage up gradually
so the heater element doesn’t overheat and burn out.

Cold side. The battery is pressed directly against the barrier’s cold face. Use a fully discharged
cell or an inert dummy cell, not a charged one (see Safety). The battery thermocouple sits on the
face touching the barrier, which is the cell’s hottest point. That makes the reading conservative,
which is the right direction for this test.

Spacing. Because the heater and battery press directly on the barrier, the gap between them
equals the barrier’s thickness. Thinner barriers therefore put the heat source closer to the cell.
That’s realistic, since a thinner barrier in a module saves space, but keep it in mind when
comparing materials of different thicknesses. Intumescents will expand against the sand rather
than into open space, which is more confining than a real module. Note this as well.

Thermocouples. There are two: one on the heater and one on the battery, each on the side facing
the barrier. Mount them at the same height, directly across the barrier from each other, so both
measure the same heat path. Use a mineral-insulated K-type probe for the heater thermocouple,
because the fiberglass-insulated kind fails above about 480 °C. Glass-insulated wire is fine for
the battery thermocouple, held on with Kapton tape. Read both through MAX31856 boards at 1
Hz on the ESP32, which now only logs data and doesn’t control the heater. Your detection
subgroup can still reuse this data.

samples.

Procedure for each run

   1.​ Weigh the barrier sample and measure its thickness at several points.
   2.​ Check that the plastic wrap and tape on the bottom of the cinderblock are intact.
   3.​ Clamp the barrier in the middle of the cavity.
   4.​ Press the heater against the hot face and the battery against the cold face. Check that both
       thermocouples are seated and level with each other.
   5.​ Fill each side with the same mass of dry sand, packed the same way as previous runs.
   6.​ Log 2 minutes of baseline.
   7.​ Bring the variac up to reach setpoint. Start the 30-minute clock when the heater
       thermocouple reaches it.
   8.​ If the battery thermocouple reaches 150 °C, cut power and record the time. That’s a failed
       barrier, and heating further adds risk without adding information.
   9.​ At 30 minutes, cut heater power and keep logging until the battery temperature has
       peaked and begun to fall. The sand stores heat and keeps feeding it to the battery after the
       heater is off, so this may take longer than the 10 minutes you used before.
   10.​After cooldown, remove the sand and gently brush the barrier clean. Weigh it again. The
       mass loss tells you how much gas it released, which matters for your purge subgroup.
       Photograph it and measure any char thickness or cracking.

Analysis

Plot battery temperature against time for every barrier on one graph, with a line at 150 °C. That
single figure carries most of your story. Then make a scatter plot of t₁₅₀ or peak battery
temperature against areal density, so the lightest effective barriers stand out in one corner.


===== PDF PAGE 35 =====
Finally, translate each result to the pack level. Barrier mass per cell divided by an assumed cell
mass gives a parasitic mass fraction, and that converts directly into a Wh/kg penalty.

Safety

Use a discharged or dummy battery. If a charged lithium cell gets hot enough, it can go into real
thermal runaway and vent flammable, toxic gas inside a cardboard box. Even with a discharged
cell, stop at 150 °C on the battery thermocouple.

Run tests in a fume hood or outdoors. Hot silicone, intumescents, and graphite release smoke and
gases, sometimes including ammonia.

Keep the heater leads and anything hot away from the cardboard. Cardboard can ignite well
below your 600 °C setpoint. The plastic wrap on the bottom will melt at roughly 100 °C, so
check it after each run and replace it if it’s softened. A tray under the box will catch sand if the
seal fails.

Variac-powered cartridge heaters run on 120 V. Have someone qualified check the wiring, plug
in through a GFCI, and keep a kill switch within reach. Ideally, add an independent
over-temperature cutoff on the heater too. Keep an extinguisher and a bucket of dry sand nearby,
and never leave a run unattended.

Option A: heater coil wrapped around a real cell 1

Pros

   ●​ Realistic geometry and materials on both sides.
   ●​ Cells are cheap (the 25R is $4.99), and the setup is simple to build.

Cons

   ●​ It can’t get hot enough. A charged cell starts heating itself at roughly 80–120 °C, so to stay
      safely out of runaway you’d have to stay below that. Your barrier would then be holding back less
      than 100 °C instead of a runaway-like 500 °C or more, which makes the test far too easy and not
      representative.

   ●​ Discharging cell 1 doesn’t fully fix it. An empty cell won’t run away violently, but well above
      150 °C it will still vent flammable electrolyte vapor.
   ●​ The coil sits between the cell and the wall. It changes the contact you’re trying to measure, and
      the coil runs hotter than the cell, so you can’t be sure what temperature the barrier actually sees.

Pros

   ●​ It’s reusable, with no hazardous chemistry.

   ●​ Not a real cell. There’s no internal heat generation, venting, or flame, the same limitation as the
      block design. Note it in your report.

Acceptance Criteria

Test setup (apply to every run)

   ●​ Cell 1 (heat source): a steel rod heated by a cartridge heater, standing in for a cell in
      thermal runaway. It is held at T2 = 500°C ±10°C, measured on the rod surface at the
      barrier.
   ●​ Cell 2: a Samsung [model] lithium-ion cell at [100]% state of charge. Use a fresh cell for
      every run.
   ●​ Hold time: the 30-minute clock starts when T2 first reaches 500°C.
   ●​ Kept the same every run: barrier build, sand fill, clamping and spacers, thermocouple
      attachment, outer insulation wrap, and starting temperature (room temperature ±[3]°C).
   ●​ Invalid runs: a run doesn’t count if T2 leaves the 490–510°C range after reaching
      setpoint, or if a thermocouple comes loose. Treat either as a setup problem, not a barrier
      failure.

1. The barrier prevents thermal runaway from spreading to cell 2 for 30 minutes

   ●​ Definition: thermal runaway is when a cell’s internal chemical reactions produce heat
      faster than it can escape. The cell then keeps heating itself, ending in venting, fire or
      rupture. In this test, the barrier must keep cell 2 below the temperature where its own
      self-heating would begin.
   ●​ Pass, all of the following for the full 30-minute hold:
          ○​ T3 (cell 2 surface at the barrier) stays below [100°C].
                  ■​ Why 100°C: in lithium-ion cells, the protective layer on the anode starts
                       breaking down at about 80–120°C, separators begin melting at about
                       130°C, and self-heating usually starts somewhere between 90 and 150°C.
                       A 100°C limit leaves margin. Confirm it against the Samsung
                       datasheet.
          ○​ Cell 2 shows no venting, smoke, swelling or fire.
   ●​ On failure: record the time at which T3 crossed the limit, or when venting started.

2. The barrier is still usable after one run

   ●​ Reusable vs. consumable parts:
         ○​ The mica box is expected to be reusable.
         ○​ [The aerogel wrap is replaced each run, or is expected to be reusable; decide
              which.]
   ●​ Burn-in first: do the “before” measurements after one burn-in run, because the first
      heating burns off the mica’s binder.
   ●​ Pass, for the reusable parts:
         ○​ No through-cracks, holes or peeling layers on the barrier face, documented with
              before-and-after photos at the same angle and lighting.
         ○​ The box holds its shape and can be taken apart and reassembled without breaking.

3. The setup and results are reproducible

   ●​ Number of runs: at least 3 valid runs with the same configuration, each with a fresh
      Samsung cell.
   ●​ Main metric: the rise in T3 over the hold, ΔT3 = T3 at 30 min − T3 at start. Each run’s
      ΔT3 must be within ±10% of the average of all runs.
         ○​ Use the temperature rise rather than the raw T3 reading, because a percentage of a
            raw °C value depends on the starting temperature.
   ●​ Secondary metric: the time for T3 to reach [50°C] is within ±10% across runs.

Safety requirements (before any run with a real cell)

   ●​ Location: outdoors or in a fume hood or fire-rated enclosure. A cell in a runaway
      releases flammable, toxic gases, including hydrogen fluoride.
   ●​ Manual monitoring: T2, T3 and cell 1’s voltage are logged remotely, and operators will
      open-control
   ●​ Fire response: plenty of water ready nearby for cooling the cell.

Limitations (state in the report)

   ●​ Heat only: the heater reproduces conducted heat only. It does not vent gas, eject material
      or produce flame jets like a real cell in a runaway, so the test doesn’t show how the
      barrier handles those.
   ●​ Lower peak, longer hold: 500°C is below the 600–800°C+ peak a real Samsung cell can
      reach. But holding it for 30 minutes delivers far more total heat than a real event, where
      the peak lasts only minutes. Overall, the test is conservative for heat soaking through the
      barrier.
